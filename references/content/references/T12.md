# T12 · YMYL trust block (conditional: triggered by `ymyl=true`)

> Trigger: [T4](T4.md) marked `ymyl: true` · Downstream: [C21 YMYL trust block](../../checklist/references/C21.md) · red line **R2**

## Introduction

YMYL = Your Money or Your Life. Insurance content trips it nearly across the board — readers
make decisions on what you write, and a wrong decision genuinely loses money or delays care.

For this kind of content, search engines and AI don't ask whether the writing itself is good;
they ask: **why should anyone believe you?** The answer must be verifiable: who wrote it,
what qualifies them, who reviewed it, when it was reviewed, and on what sources.

The whole set must satisfy **two faces at once**:

- **the visible face** — readers can see the byline, the review note, the source links on the page
- **the machine face** — `author` / `reviewedBy` / `citation` in the JSON-LD match the visible face **word for word**

C21 is a human-review item, and what it reviews is exactly whether these two faces line up.

## How to deliver

```yaml
ymyl: true

author:
  name: Zhang Ming
  title: Licensed insurance advisor
  credential: California Insurance License #0M12345
  bio_url: /team/zhang-ming          # a dedicated author page is best

reviewer:
  name: Dr. Sarah Chen
  title: MD, Internal Medicine
  reviewed_on: 2026-08-20            # the date the review actually happened

citations:
  - https://www.uscis.gov/…          # official sources
  - https://www.healthcare.gov/…
```

### Three hard requirements

1. **Author: real name + title + verifiable credential.** "The Acme editorial team" is
   not an author — with no specific person, there is no verifiable expertise.

2. **The review must actually happen.** This is the **R2 red line**, not a formatting rule.
   Writing "Medically reviewed by Dr. X" when Dr. X never read the piece is fabricated
   credential endorsement — a substantive risk at the legal and platform level, not
   over-eager SEO. **Better no reviewer at all than one who never reviewed.**

3. **Citations from official sources.** Government sites (uscis.gov, irs.gov, healthcare.gov),
   regulators, academic institutions, insurers' official policy pages.

## Common mistakes

- **Crediting a review team that doesn't exist** — see above; this is a red line, not a mistake
- **The review date filled with the publish date** — fake at a glance; a real review gets the real date, and no review means no field
- **Citing your own blog as a source** — circular citation, zero authority gained; citations must point at authority **outside yourself**
- **Citing news coverage instead of the primary document** — policy content cites the original uscis.gov page, not some outlet's report on it
- **An author with a name but no credential** — "written by Zhang Ming" builds no E-E-A-T; neither readers nor machines know why he understands insurance
- **Title inflation** — "Chief Insurance Expert" loses to "licensed insurance advisor + license number"; **verifiable > impressive-sounding**
